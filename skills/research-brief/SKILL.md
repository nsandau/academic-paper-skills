# research-brief

## Purpose

Answer a well-defined research question quickly enough for preliminary orientation, planning, meetings, or deciding whether deeper research is warranted.

This skill preserves the useful core of the upstream `quick` research mode without pretending that a small source set is a comprehensive literature review.

## Workflow

### 1. Bound the question
State the question, population or context, relevant period, and what type of answer is needed.

### 2. Run a focused search
Prefer a small set of highly relevant sources rather than a large noisy list. A typical brief may rely on roughly 5 to 8 strong sources, but relevance and evidence diversity matter more than hitting a quota.

### 3. Verify sources
Check bibliographic identity and publication status. For central claims, distinguish full-text evidence from abstract-only or metadata-only evidence.

### 4. Synthesize rather than list
Identify:
- the most stable findings
- important disagreements
- major methodological differences
- likely boundary conditions
- what remains uncertain

### 5. State the search boundary
Record enough about the search to make clear why this is preliminary. Do not label it systematic or exhaustive.

## Output

### Research Question

### Bottom Line
A short answer calibrated to the evidence.

### Evidence Summary
Organize by themes or claims rather than paper-by-paper summaries.

### Important Disagreements or Caveats

### Source Table
For each central source include relevance, design, population or corpus, main finding, and read scope.

### What This Brief Cannot Establish
State coverage, search, access, and evidence limitations.

### If Deeper Research Is Needed
Identify which questions need a full literature search, methodology review, systematic review, or additional primary sources.

## Boundaries

- Do not call the brief comprehensive.
- Do not infer absence of evidence from a rapid search.
- Do not use weakly verified sources for headline conclusions when stronger sources are available.
