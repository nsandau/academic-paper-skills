# research-design

## Purpose

Design a methodology blueprint or critique an existing design. The central rule is that the question drives the method.

## Methodology alignment

Start by restating the research question and identifying what kind of inference it requires.

Typical mappings include:
- Descriptive questions: surveys, descriptive datasets, case description, content analysis
- Comparative questions: comparative cases, cross-sectional designs, benchmarking
- Association questions: correlational designs, regression, longitudinal association models
- Causal questions: experiments, quasi-experiments, natural experiments, stronger identification strategies
- Experience or meaning questions: interviews, ethnography, phenomenology, grounded theory, narrative methods
- Evaluation questions: program evaluation, policy analysis, cost-effectiveness, implementation evaluation

Treat these as starting points, not mandatory templates.

## Design components

### Paradigm and inference assumptions
Make philosophical assumptions explicit only when they matter to design or interpretation. Do not force paradigm language into fields where it adds no value.

### Method
Specify the design and why it can answer the RQ better than realistic alternatives.

### Data strategy
Specify:
- Primary, secondary, or mixed data
- Population or corpus
- Sampling or case-selection logic
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria
- Time frame
- Measurement or coding strategy
- Missing-data or unavailable-data concerns

### Analysis
Specify:
- Main analytical technique
- Model or coding logic
- Important assumptions
- Confirmatory and exploratory distinctions where relevant
- Robustness and sensitivity checks

### Validity and quality
For quantitative work consider internal validity, external validity, measurement validity, reliability, model assumptions, and identification threats.

For qualitative work consider credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability, reflexivity, and saturation where appropriate.

For mixed methods consider integration quality and whether the strands actually answer complementary parts of the RQ.

### Reporting standards
When applicable, identify relevant reporting guidance such as PRISMA, CONSORT, STROBE, COREQ, or SQUIRE. Treat current venue and field requirements as something to verify, not assume.

## Critique mode

When reviewing an existing design, organize findings around:
- Can the design answer the RQ?
- What assumptions are needed for the intended inference?
- Which threats are fatal, repairable, or merely limiting?
- Are claims likely to exceed what the design can support?
- What additional analysis would materially change confidence?

## Output

### Methodology Blueprint
- RQ and inference target
- Design
- Justification relative to alternatives
- Data and sampling
- Measures or coding
- Analysis plan
- Assumptions
- Validity and quality strategy
- Robustness or sensitivity checks
- Limitations by design
- Reporting standard if applicable
- Unresolved methodological decisions

If critiquing, add a prioritized list of design problems with exact consequences for inference.

## General guardrails

- Do not invent sources, citations, quotations, data, results, reviewer comments, or journal requirements.
- Distinguish what is supplied by the user, what is directly verified, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.
- Do not claim a search was exhaustive unless the search process justifies that claim.
- Do not treat source existence as proof that a source supports a particular claim.
- Do not silently strengthen association into causation, a local result into a universal claim, or exploratory evidence into confirmatory evidence.
- If required material is missing, identify the gap and continue with the parts that can be assessed.
- Prefer traceable reasoning. Important findings should point to the exact claim, section, source, table, figure, or reviewer comment that triggered them.
