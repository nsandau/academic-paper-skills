# research-gap

## Purpose

Find defensible research gaps without equating `I did not find it` with `it has never been studied`.

## Gap types

- Empirical: a relevant phenomenon or relationship lacks adequate evidence
- Theoretical: existing theory does not explain an observed pattern or important construct relationship
- Methodological: evidence depends heavily on one design, measure, model, or data source
- Population: important groups are missing or underrepresented
- Geographic or contextual: evidence is concentrated in particular settings
- Temporal: evidence is outdated for a changing phenomenon
- Contradictory: credible studies produce materially inconsistent results
- Replication: influential findings lack independent or contextually meaningful replication
- Integration: separate literatures address connected parts of the problem without being synthesized

## Gap test

For each candidate gap ask:
1. What exactly is missing?
2. What is the nearest prior work?
3. Is the gap important to the RQ or merely easy to state?
4. Could the apparent gap be a search failure or terminology mismatch?
5. Does filling the gap produce a substantive contribution?
6. Is the claim about absence appropriately qualified?

## Novelty wording ladder

Prefer the weakest wording that the search can defend:
- `Few studies in the reviewed corpus...`
- `Existing studies identified here concentrate on...`
- `We found limited evidence addressing...`
- `No eligible study was identified in the documented search...`

Reserve universal claims such as `no prior study has...` for unusually strong documented evidence.

## Output

### Gap Analysis
For each gap:
- Gap type
- Exact gap claim
- Evidence supporting the claim
- Nearest prior work
- Search limitations
- Why the gap matters
- Research question it could motivate
- Confidence in the gap claim
- Safer wording if current wording overreaches

## General guardrails

- Do not invent sources, citations, quotations, data, results, reviewer comments, or journal requirements.
- Distinguish what is supplied by the user, what is directly verified, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.
- Do not claim a search was exhaustive unless the search process justifies that claim.
- Do not treat source existence as proof that a source supports a particular claim.
- Do not silently strengthen association into causation, a local result into a universal claim, or exploratory evidence into confirmatory evidence.
- If required material is missing, identify the gap and continue with the parts that can be assessed.
- Prefer traceable reasoning. Important findings should point to the exact claim, section, source, table, figure, or reviewer comment that triggered them.
