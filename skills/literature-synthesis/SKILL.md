# literature-synthesis

## Purpose

Perform the intellectual step between finding literature and writing a literature review. Merge the repo's synthesis agent and cross-paper comparison logic.

## Workflow

### 1. Build a study matrix
For each source record what matters to comparison, such as question, population, design, method, measures, context, findings, limitations, and evidence quality.

### 2. Cluster by substantive question
Group studies by themes or mechanisms, not merely by publication date or author.

### 3. Map convergence
Identify findings that are reproduced across meaningfully comparable studies. Weight convergence by design quality and relevance, not source count alone.

### 4. Map divergence
For apparent contradictions, ask whether studies actually differ in:
- Population
- Context
- Time period
- Operationalization
- Intervention or exposure
- Analytical method
- Study quality
- Theoretical assumptions

Do not manufacture contradictions between studies that do not address the same question.

### 5. Identify boundary conditions
State when a finding appears to hold and where evidence is absent or inconsistent.

### 6. Integrate theory
Show whether evidence supports, refines, contradicts, or sits outside the relevant theoretical framework.

### 7. Identify gaps
Separate empirical, methodological, theoretical, temporal, geographic, population, and replication gaps.

## Writing rule

Avoid sequential summaries such as `Study A found... Study B found...`. Use sources as evidence inside thematic claims. Paper-by-paper detail is appropriate only when a particular study is pivotal or methodologically distinctive.

## Output

### Synthesis Report
- Scope and corpus limitations
- Major themes
- Stable findings
- Contested findings
- Contradictions and plausible resolutions
- Boundary conditions
- Methodological patterns
- Theoretical integration
- Knowledge gaps
- Implications for the user's RQ
- Synthesis limitations

Include a compact evidence matrix when it materially improves traceability.

## General guardrails

- Do not invent sources, citations, quotations, data, results, reviewer comments, or journal requirements.
- Distinguish what is supplied by the user, what is directly verified, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.
- Do not claim a search was exhaustive unless the search process justifies that claim.
- Do not treat source existence as proof that a source supports a particular claim.
- Do not silently strengthen association into causation, a local result into a universal claim, or exploratory evidence into confirmatory evidence.
- If required material is missing, identify the gap and continue with the parts that can be assessed.
- Prefer traceable reasoning. Important findings should point to the exact claim, section, source, table, figure, or reviewer comment that triggered them.
